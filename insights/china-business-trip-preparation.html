Trip Preparation · Pre-Departure Briefing

How to prepare for a business trip to China: the 2026 checklist.

Most China trips do not fail on the agenda — they fail on the small things nobody checked. This is the full pre-departure briefing we run with every client, in the order we run it.

By Sino Business Partner Updated August 2026 9 min read Trip Preparation
Key facts, in short
  • Start 6–8 weeks out. Visa processing, invitation letters and meeting confirmations all need lead time.
  • Entry rules depend on your passport. Around 50 countries have 30-day unilateral visa-free entry; 55+ qualify for the 240-hour (10-day) visa-free transit. Israeli passport holders need a visa.
  • Set up Alipay and WeChat Pay before you fly. Foreign Visa and Mastercard now link to both after a passport verification. China is effectively cashless.
  • Assume your usual apps are blocked. Arrange an international eSIM or roaming plan, and install WeChat before departure.
  • Avoid Golden Week — the holiday periods around the first days of May and October. Flights, hotels and factories all go sideways.
  • Bring double the business cards you think you need, and print one side in Simplified Chinese.

A business trip to China rewards preparation more than almost any other destination. Not because it is difficult — the infrastructure is world-class and the country is exceptionally safe — but because the operating system is different. Payments, communications, transport booking and meeting culture all work on rails that most Western travelers have never used.

The good news: every one of those gaps can be closed before you board. Here is the sequence.

The 8-week timeline

Working backwards from departure is the single most useful thing you can do. Visa appointments and factory confirmations are the two items that most often slip.

Visa & entry: it depends entirely on your passport

China has expanded entry access substantially, but the rules are not uniform. Three tracks matter:

  • Unilateral visa-free entry (about 50 countries) — typically 30 days, covering most EU states, the UK, Canada and Australia. The United States is not currently on this list.
  • 240-hour visa-free transit (55+ countries, including the US) — up to 10 days, provided you enter and exit through eligible ports and hold an onward ticket to a third country. Excellent for a focused trip; restrictive if your itinerary changes.
  • A standard business (M) or tourist (L) visa — required for Israeli passport holders and anyone outside the above schemes. Multi-entry, long-validity options are widely available and worth requesting if you expect to return.
Verify before you book

Entry policies have changed repeatedly over the past two years, and eligibility can depend on your specific port of entry. Confirm your situation with the Chinese embassy or consulate in your country — or ask us, and we will confirm it for your passport and route before anything is booked.

Money & payments: solve this before you land

China is effectively cashless. Taxis, restaurants, metro gates, market stalls and even temple donation boxes run on QR codes. Cards are accepted in international hotels and little else.

Both Alipay and WeChat Pay now accept foreign Visa and Mastercard after a short in-app passport verification. Set up both — some merchants accept only one — and complete verification at home, where you still have unrestricted internet and a stable phone number for SMS codes.

  • Carry a modest amount of RMB cash as backup for small independent vendors and rural stops.
  • Tell your bank you are traveling; unexpected China transactions are a common fraud trigger.
  • Tipping is not customary in mainland China and can cause mild awkwardness. Hotel porters are the practical exception.

Connectivity: plan for the gap

A number of Western services — including Google, WhatsApp, Instagram and many news sites — are not reachable on the standard mainland internet. This trips up more business travelers than any other single issue, because it breaks email, calendar and navigation simultaneously.

Three workable approaches, in order of reliability:

  1. International roaming or an eSIM from your home carrier. Traffic routes outside the mainland, so your usual apps generally keep working. The simplest option, and the one we recommend to most clients.
  2. A reputable VPN installed and tested before departure — you cannot reliably download one after arrival. Treat it as a backup, not a primary plan.
  3. Adopt the local stack. Install WeChat, use Baidu Maps or Amap, and rely on Chinese-hosted services. This is what your counterparts use, and it works flawlessly.

Whatever else you do: install WeChat and complete verification before you fly. New accounts sometimes require a scan-based vouch from an existing user, which is much easier to arrange while you are still home. On the ground WeChat is not a social app — it is how business is conducted, contacts are exchanged, meetings are confirmed and payments are made.

Meetings & etiquette: the signals that matter

Chinese business culture is warm, hierarchical and highly attentive to form. You do not need to perform the customs perfectly — visible effort is what registers.

Business cards

Offer and receive with both hands, Chinese side facing the recipient. Read the card for a moment before setting it on the table in front of you — not into your pocket, and never into a back pocket. Bring far more than you expect to use.

Seniority and seating

The most senior person often speaks least. Watch who the room defers to. At meals, seating is by rank, and the host will direct you — let them.

The banquet is the meeting

Dinner is not an add-on to the negotiation; it is frequently where the relationship is actually built. Accept the invitation. Expect toasts. Pace yourself — and if you do not drink, say so early, warmly, and with a reason (medical or medication works well and is respected).

Reading "yes"

A "yes" in a Chinese meeting often means "I have understood you" rather than "I agree." Direct refusal is avoided because it costs face. Confirm every substantive point afterwards in writing, in friendly terms — that is normal and expected, not distrustful.

Gifts

A small, well-presented gift from your home country lands well. Avoid clocks, sharp objects, white or black wrapping and anything in fours — all carry unlucky associations. Expect gifts to be declined once or twice before being accepted; offer again.

On-the-ground logistics

  • Trains beat planes for most domestic legs under about 1,000 km. China's high-speed rail network exceeds 40,000 km at speeds up to 350 km/h, runs precisely on time, and drops you in city centres rather than distant airports.
  • Book rail in advance. Popular business routes sell out, and you need your passport number to book and to collect tickets.
  • Arrive at stations early. Chinese rail stations are airport-scale, with security screening and ID checks.
  • Use DiDi for taxis (it has an English interface) and save your destinations in Chinese characters to show drivers.
  • Hotels must register foreign guests. Stick to properties licensed to accept international visitors — every major chain and most business hotels are.
  • Emergency numbers: 110 police · 120 medical · 119 fire.

What to actually pack

  • Passport, visa, printed invitation letter and hotel confirmations — paper copies still resolve disputes fastest.
  • Business cards, bilingual, in quantity.
  • Universal adapter (China uses 220V; sockets are usually Type A/I).
  • Power bank — you will navigate, translate and pay from your phone all day.
  • Any prescription medication in original packaging with a copy of the prescription.
  • Modest formal business attire; China is more conservative than most Western business settings.
  • Comfortable shoes. Trade fairs and factory tours involve serious walking.

Five avoidable mistakes

  1. Booking a hotel by price rather than district. In cities this size, the wrong neighbourhood costs you 90 minutes a day.
  2. Arriving with no interpreter for technical meetings. Your counterpart's English may be excellent socially and thin on specification detail — exactly where errors are expensive.
  3. Scheduling back-to-back meetings across a city. Traffic and scale are unforgiving. Three well-spaced meetings beat six rushed ones.
  4. Treating the trip as purely transactional. Relationships in China are built over meals and time, and they are what make the second order happen.
  5. Flying home the moment business ends. One extra day — the Great Wall, the Bund, a tea house — is what the trip is remembered for, and what makes the next one easy to approve.

None of this is complicated. It is simply a different set of defaults, and every item above is closed with a checklist and a bit of lead time. That is precisely the work we do for clients before they fly — so that the only thing left to think about on arrival is the meeting itself.

Frequently asked questions

How far in advance should I plan a business trip to China?

Start six to eight weeks before departure. Visa processing, invitation letters and meeting confirmations all need lead time, and popular high-speed rail routes and hotels near major trade fairs sell out early.

Do I need a visa for China in 2026?

It depends on your passport. Around 50 countries — including most EU states, the UK, Canada and Australia — have 30-day unilateral visa-free entry. More than 55 countries, including the United States, qualify for the 240-hour (10-day) visa-free transit scheme when entering and exiting through eligible ports with an onward ticket to a third country. Israeli passport holders require a visa, though multi-entry long-validity options are available. Because these policies change, confirm with your local Chinese embassy or consulate before booking.

Can I use my Visa or Mastercard in China?

Yes, but mainly through mobile apps rather than physical card terminals. Foreign Visa and Mastercard now link to both Alipay and WeChat Pay after a passport verification in the app. China is effectively cashless, so set both apps up and verify them before you travel, and carry some RMB cash for small independent vendors.

Will WhatsApp and Google work in China?

Not on the standard mainland internet. Google services, WhatsApp, Instagram and many Western sites are not reachable. The most reliable solution is international roaming or an eSIM from your home carrier, which routes traffic outside the mainland. Install WeChat before you fly — it is the primary business communication tool in China.

When is the best time to visit China for business?

Spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November) offer the mildest weather and clearest skies. Avoid the Golden Week holiday periods around the first days of May and October, when domestic travel peaks and many factories and offices close.

Do I need an interpreter for business meetings in China?

For technical, contractual or negotiation-heavy meetings, yes. Counterparts may speak strong conversational English but lack precise vocabulary for specifications, tolerances or legal terms — exactly where misunderstandings become expensive. For social and introductory meetings an interpreter is often unnecessary.
