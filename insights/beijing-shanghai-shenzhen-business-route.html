Routes · The Working Week

Beijing–Shanghai–Shenzhen: the 7-day business route.

Policy, capital and hardware — three ecosystems in one working week. A route we build often, with the transport, districts and timing that make it survivable.

By Sino Business Partner Updated August 2026 7 min read Routes
Key facts, in short
  • Seven days, three cities — Beijing (policy, HQs, state enterprises), Shanghai (finance, international business), Shenzhen (hardware, electronics, manufacturing).
  • Beijing to Shanghai is best by high-speed rail — roughly 1,300 km in about 4.5–6 hours, city centre to city centre.
  • Shanghai to Shenzhen is best by air — roughly two hours; the rail option is considerably longer.
  • Build in one bleisure morning per city. It costs almost nothing in working time and it is what the trip is remembered for.
  • Three well-spaced meetings a day beats six rushed ones. These cities are large and traffic is unforgiving.
  • Best seasons: spring (Mar–May) and autumn (Sep–Nov). Avoid Golden Week in early May and early October.

Why this route

China is not one market, and a trip that treats it as one usually underdelivers. These three cities are chosen because they represent genuinely different ecosystems, and because the transport between them is fast enough to make a single week realistic.

  • Beijing — the capital. Government and policy, corporate headquarters, state-owned enterprises, industry associations. Where strategy and permission live.
  • Shanghai — finance and international business. Multinational regional offices, flagship exhibitions, the most globally fluent boardrooms in the country.
  • Shenzhen — hardware. Electronics, manufacturing, prototyping and the fastest product-to-market ecosystem anywhere on earth.

If your business touches policy, capital and production, this is the shape of the trip. If it touches only one, take the relevant city and go deeper — see the variants below.

Days 1–2: Beijing 北京

Arrive. Beijing has two international airports — Capital (PEK) and the newer Daxing (PKX). They are far apart; confirm which one your flight uses and base your first-day timing on it.

Day 1 — land, settle, one soft meeting

Do not schedule a hard negotiation on arrival day. Long-haul plus jet lag plus an unfamiliar operating system is not the condition in which to hold your position. Use the afternoon for an introductory meeting, an association visit, or a relationship dinner.

Where to base: the CBD around Guomao for corporate meetings, or Sanlitun for a more contemporary, hospitality-led base. Both give you good metro access.

Day 2 — the working day

Two to three substantive meetings. Beijing traffic is legendary; cluster meetings geographically and use the metro for anything crossing the city at peak hours.

The free morning

The Great Wall at Mutianyu is roughly 90 minutes out of the city and is worth an early start. Alternatively, the Forbidden City and a walk through the hutong lanes near Shichahai gives you the same effect within the city and half the travel. Either way — do one of them. Clients remember this part.

Day 3: Transfer south

Take the morning high-speed train to Shanghai. The line covers roughly 1,300 km in about four and a half to six hours depending on service, and it runs to the minute. Business class is genuinely workable — a table, power, quiet — so this is a productive half-day rather than a lost one.

  • Book in advance; passport details are required for booking and collection.
  • Arrive at the station early — Chinese rail stations are airport-scale with security screening.
  • Trains from Beijing South arrive at Shanghai Hongqiao, which is well connected to the city by metro.

Arrive by early afternoon, check in, and use the late afternoon for one meeting or a site visit. Evening: the Bund. It is a cliché because it is genuinely spectacular, and it makes an excellent client dinner backdrop.

Days 4–5: Shanghai 上海

Where to base: Jing'an or the Bund/Huangpu area for a central position with strong hotel and restaurant depth; Pudong (Lujiazui) if your meetings are financial-sector and concentrated there.

Day 4 — the commercial day

Shanghai is where international-facing conversations happen most easily. English fluency is highest here, and counterparts are used to Western business norms — which does not mean the underlying culture differs, only that the interface is smoother.

Two to three meetings. If your visit coincides with a major expo at the National Exhibition and Convention Center, build a half-day around it — Shanghai hosts several of China's flagship international exhibitions.

Day 5 — depth, then the evening

Use this day for the meeting that actually matters — you are now two days into the country, adjusted, and reading the room properly. Keep the afternoon lighter.

The free evening

A Huangpu River cruise, Yu Garden, or dinner in the former French Concession. Shanghai after dark does the work of a hundred slides on why the relationship is worth continuing.

Days 6–7: Shenzhen 深圳

Getting there: fly. Shanghai to Shenzhen is about two hours in the air; the rail alternative is considerably longer and not a good use of a working week. Take an early flight and you are in a meeting by late morning.

Day 6 — the hardware day

Shenzhen is where physical products get made and iterated. Factory visits, prototyping houses, component markets. If you are sourcing or manufacturing, this is the day the trip pays for itself.

  • Confirm factory visits in writing 48 hours out and again the morning of — schedules move.
  • Bring an interpreter with technical vocabulary. This is the day where a general interpreter is not enough.
  • Allow travel time. The Pearl River Delta is dense; a "nearby" factory can still be 90 minutes away.

Day 7 — close and depart

Morning for follow-ups, contract conversations and anything that surfaced during the factory day. Fly home from Shenzhen (SZX), or cross to Hong Kong for wider long-haul options — the connection is straightforward and Hong Kong offers substantially more direct routes to Europe and North America.

If you have one extra day

Guangzhou is under an hour from Shenzhen by high-speed rail — the trade capital of the south and home of the Canton Fair. If your timing overlaps a fair phase, extending here is usually the highest-return day of the whole trip.

Route logistics

  • Rail under ~1,000 km, air beyond it. That single rule optimises most China itineraries.
  • Book hotels by district, not price. The wrong neighbourhood costs 90 minutes a day in cities this scale.
  • Keep one buffer slot per city. Meetings get moved; a buffer converts that from a crisis into an opportunity.
  • Set up Alipay, WeChat Pay and WeChat before departure — see the preparation checklist.
  • One interpreter across the whole route is usually better than three local ones. Continuity of context is worth a great deal.

This route is a template, not a prescription. The version we actually build for a client depends on who they need to meet, what they are buying or selling, and how much of the week they want back at the end of it. Tell us the goal and we will design the week around it.

Frequently asked questions

How many days do you need for a business trip to China?

Seven days is the practical minimum for covering multiple cities, allowing for long-haul flights, jet lag and meaningful meetings in each location. A single-city trip can work in four to five days. Anything shorter across multiple cities tends to produce rushed meetings and poor recall.

Should I take the train or fly between Chinese cities?

As a rule, take high-speed rail for journeys under roughly 1,000 km and fly beyond that. Beijing to Shanghai (about 1,300 km) takes roughly 4.5 to 6 hours by rail and is still often preferable because it runs city centre to city centre with workable business-class seating. Shanghai to Shenzhen is better flown, at about two hours.

Which Chinese city should I visit for my industry?

Beijing for government, policy, corporate headquarters and state-owned enterprises. Shanghai for finance, international business and flagship exhibitions. Shenzhen for electronics, hardware and manufacturing. Guangzhou for trade and importing, including the Canton Fair. Hangzhou for e-commerce and the digital economy. Yiwu for small-commodity wholesale sourcing.

How many meetings should I schedule per day in China?

Two to three substantive meetings per day is realistic. Chinese cities are large and traffic is heavy, so cluster meetings geographically and keep a buffer slot each day. Six rushed meetings produce worse outcomes than three well-spaced ones.

Is it worth adding leisure days to a China business trip?

Yes. A single free morning per city — the Great Wall in Beijing, the Bund in Shanghai — costs almost no working time and substantially improves both the client relationship and the internal case for a return trip. This bleisure model is standard practice across Asia-Pacific business travel.
